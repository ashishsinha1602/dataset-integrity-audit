\begin{abstract}
Hugging Face Spaces host interactive demonstrations of machine-learning work,
and links to them appear throughout papers, model cards, and documentation. We
enumerate the complete public catalogue---$1{,}458{,}692$ Spaces from
$778{,}898$ authors---and report the fraction that still runs.

Excluding \texttt{static} Spaces, which are files on a CDN and cannot fail,
$443{,}683$ of $1{,}150{,}413$ real applications ($38.57\%$) are in a
permanently failed state, while only $32{,}786$ ($2.85\%$) are running. A
listed non-static Space is $13.5\times$ more likely to be broken than running.

The failure rate tracks age monotonically: $85.82\%$ of the 2022 cohort is
broken, against $23.15\%$ of the 2025 cohort. This is the signature of
dependency drift rather than of demos that never worked, and it is corroborated
by modification times---$33.22\%$ of broken Spaces were last touched over two
years ago, against $7.48\%$ of healthy ones.

None of this is concealed. Hugging Face publishes the runtime stage of every
Space through its own API. The contribution here is the aggregate, which to our
knowledge has not previously been reported, and the observation that the
headline \texttt{RUNNING} count is dominated by static pages: $307{,}573$ of
the $340{,}359$ Spaces marked running are static HTML.

Data and code are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A hosted demo is a claim that a piece of work can be run. Spaces links are
cited in papers, embedded in model cards, and used as evidence that a method
is usable. That claim decays silently: the link stays valid, the page still
loads, and the application behind it stopped building some time ago.

Hugging Face reports the runtime state of every Space through its public API,
so this is not hidden information. It has, as far as we can determine, simply
not been added up.

Our contributions are a complete-population census rather than a sample, a
validation pass distinguishing permanent failure from transient states, and
the observation that the platform's own headline availability number is
carried almost entirely by static pages that cannot fail.

\section{Method}

We enumerated the public Spaces catalogue through
\texttt{HfApi.list\_spaces} with the \texttt{runtime}, \texttt{sdk},
\texttt{likes}, \texttt{createdAt}, and \texttt{lastModified} fields expanded,
paginating until the generator was exhausted. This yielded $1{,}458{,}692$
records with no duplicate identifiers and no private entries. It is the
population, not a sample, so no weighting or confidence intervals are required.

Runtime state is available in the listing itself, which is what makes a
full-population measurement practical; a per-Space query would require 1.46
million calls.

\subsection{What counts as broken}

We treat \texttt{BUILD\_ERROR}, \texttt{RUNTIME\_ERROR}, \texttt{CONFIG\_ERROR},
and \texttt{NO\_APP\_FILE} as broken. We deliberately do \emph{not} count
\texttt{SLEEPING}: a sleeping Space wakes on request and is working software.
Folding sleeping Spaces into a defect count would roughly double the headline
figure and would be wrong.

\subsection{Static Spaces are excluded from the denominator}

A \texttt{static} Space is HTML served from a CDN. It has no build step and no
runtime, so it cannot enter a failed state---their observed breakage rate is
$0.06\%$. Including them in an availability figure measures the CDN rather than
the demos. All headline figures below are computed over non-static Spaces; both
denominators are reported so either can be recovered.

\subsection{Validation}

Two checks were run against the live API rather than the listing.

\textbf{Listing fidelity.} 300 randomly selected Spaces were re-queried
individually. 299 of 300 agreed with the listing. The single disagreement was
\texttt{RUNNING} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{SLEEPING}, an ordinary idle transition.
No error state was misreported.

\textbf{Failure persistence.} 300 Spaces in error states (100 each of
\texttt{BUILD\_ERROR}, \texttt{RUNTIME\_ERROR}, \texttt{CONFIG\_ERROR}) were
independently re-queried with retries. All 300 were still broken; none had
recovered. These are not transient crashes.

\subsection{Attention is concentrated; authorship is not}

$1{,}359{,}189$ Spaces ($93.18\%$) have zero likes, and the top $0.1\%$ hold
$65.13\%$ of all $1{,}102{,}567$ likes.

Authorship shows the opposite shape, and we report it because we expected
otherwise: the ten largest authors hold $6{,}859$ Spaces, just $0.47\%$ of the
catalogue, and $518{,}796$ authors ($66.61\%$) have exactly one. The catalogue
is not the product of bulk uploaders.

\section{Limitations}

This is a single snapshot taken on 28 August 2026. The age gradient is
cross-sectional: we observe that older cohorts are more broken, not individual
Spaces breaking over time. That distinction matters, and a longitudinal panel
would be needed to close it.

We measured API-reported state, not HTTP reachability. A Space marked
\texttt{RUNNING} was not confirmed to serve a response, so the true working
fraction is at most $2.85\%$ of non-static Spaces and could be lower.

\texttt{PAUSED} is ambiguous---it may reflect a deliberate author decision or
an exhausted quota. We report it separately and exclude it from both the broken
and running counts rather than guessing.

Nothing here is concealed by the platform: every figure derives from state
Hugging Face publishes through its own API.

\section{Conclusion}

Of $1{,}150{,}413$ hosted applications on Hugging Face Spaces, $38.57\%$ are
permanently broken and $2.85\%$ are running. The platform's $340{,}359$
\texttt{RUNNING} Spaces are $90\%$ static pages that cannot fail.

The practical consequence is for anyone treating a Spaces link as evidence.
For a demo published in 2022, the prior that it still runs is about one in
seven. Link rot is understood for web citations; the same decay applies to
hosted demos, is measurable, and is worse than the raw availability figure
suggests.
